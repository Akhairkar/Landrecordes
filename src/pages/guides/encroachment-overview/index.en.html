This is general educational information, not legal advice. In encroachment cases time matters most: some legal routes stay open only for a few months after dispossession. Start the steps below early and consult a qualified lawyer and the local revenue office.

First: don't use force

Breaking locks, violence or demolishing construction yourself can create a case against you. The law aims to stop self-help, so courts listen better to someone who used legal channels.

1. Identify the type of case

  • Fresh dispossession: you were in possession and were recently pushed out. The date matters a great deal (see Section 6 below).
  • Boundary dispute: a neighbour moved the bund or wall. An official demarcation decides where the boundary is.
  • Family/co-owner dispute: co-sharers or heirs occupy more than their share. Partition is a different route.
  • Government or gram sabha land: complaints go to the revenue department.

2. Prepare proof of your rights

  • Sale deed or proof of inheritance.
  • Latest khatauni/jamabandi/7-12 and the mutation record (what is mutation). Get certified copies (online vs certified copy).
  • Land revenue/tax receipts, utility bills and the village map.

3. Collect evidence of possession and dates

  • Dated photos/videos (before and after), names and addresses of witnesses.
  • A written, step-by-step account of when and how the encroachment happened. That date will matter in every complaint and suit.
  • Bills for cultivation, sowing or repairs showing the land was in your possession.

4. If the boundary is unclear, get it measured

If the dispute is about "how much land", apply at the tehsil for measurement/demarcation. Revenue staff fix the boundary from the map and their report becomes evidence. A template is in our application drafts tool. For a rough estimate first, measure the field with GPS, but that is not an official measurement.

5. Written complaints: police, tehsil, SDM

  • Police station: forcibly entering your land is described as criminal trespass in Section 329 of the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita (BNS), which replaced IPC sections 441–448. File a written complaint and keep the receipt.
  • Tehsil/revenue officer: for record and measurement issues.
  • SDM (Executive Magistrate): if the land dispute risks a clash, under Section 164 of the Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita (BNSS), replacing CrPC 145, the magistrate summons both sides and looks at who is actually in possession. This keeps the peace; it does not decide title.

We took the BNS and BNSS text from private bare-act websites; check the official text on India Code or with a lawyer before quoting it in an application.

6. Section 6: a suit within six months of dispossession

Section 6 of the Specific Relief Act, 1963, as quoted in a Delhi High Court judgment, says:

  • A person dispossessed of immovable property without consent and otherwise than in due course of law may sue to recover possession, whatever title the other side sets up.
  • The suit cannot be brought after six months from dispossession, or against the Government.
  • No appeal or review lies from an order or decree in such a suit.
  • It does not bar a separate suit to establish title and recover possession.

In plain words: if you were in possession and were recently dispossessed, this is the fast route within six months, where the main question is who was in possession before. After six months you would sue on title, which has its own limitation period.

What the application should contain

  • Applicant's name, address, phone and land details (village, tehsil, khasra/survey, khata, area).
  • The encroacher's name (if known), date and manner of encroachment.
  • List of documents proving your rights and possession.
  • The action you want, in plain words.

For a ready structure, make an encroachment complaint draft and fill in your facts.

An example

A neighbour fences off a corner of a farmer's field on 15 March. The farmer takes photos the same day, notes witnesses, files written complaints at the police station and tehsil with receipts, and applies for demarcation. When the risk of a clash grows, the farmer applies to the SDM, and asks a lawyer whether to file a Section 6 suit before six months run out (mid-September) or sue on title. The biggest mistake would be waiting months hoping to "settle it by talking".

Keep in mind

  • Ancestral or joint land, tenancy and government land can follow different rules.
  • The encroacher may claim rights too; then dated evidence and revenue records matter even more.
  • We promise no outcome or timeline; every case turns on its facts.
