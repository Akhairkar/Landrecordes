Two contested mutation situations are the most common: someone is trying to get their name entered on your land, or your own application was rejected. In both, written papers and timely action matter most. This is general information, not legal advice; time limits and appeal routes follow state law.

Part 1: objecting to someone else's mutation

How you find out

  • A public notice in the village/tehsil, or a notice in your name.
  • A pending case showing when you look up your khasra on the portal.
  • Word from the revenue official or neighbours.

Checking your land record on the state portal from time to time is the best precaution.

What to write in the objection

  1. Case/application number (if known), village, khasra/khata number.
  2. Who you are and your link to the land: owner, co-sharer, heir or buyer.
  3. A clear reason: the deed looks forged, an heir was left out, the share is wrong, or the matter is pending in court.
  4. A list and copies of your papers: old khatauni, sale deed, succession proof, court order.
  5. What you ask for: stop the mutation and give you a hearing.

File it at the office handling the case and get a receipt. For a structure, fill your facts into the application drafts tool.

For the hearing

  • Appear on every date yourself or through a representative; in your absence a decision can be made without you.
  • Carry originals; submit copies.
  • Name any witnesses in advance.

Part 2: if your mutation is rejected

  1. Get a certified copy of the order: seeing "Rejected" on the portal is not enough; the written order gives the reasons.
  2. Identify the reason: a missing paper, a name/area mismatch, an accepted objection, or a question of title?
  3. Missing paper: fixing it and applying again is the usual route; attach the earlier order.
  4. Objection or title: the appeal or revision route lies in the state land revenue law. Time limits are often short, so consult a lawyer as soon as you get the order.
  5. Record error: get the record corrected first, then the mutation.

What not to do in either case

  • Don't rely on verbal assurances; get everything in writing with receipts.
  • Don't pay middlemen to "get it done".
  • Don't use force or quarrel; if needed see the encroachment guide.

Example

A family learned that an uncle had applied to enter only his name on all the ancestral land. That week they filed a written objection at the tehsil with the grandfather's khatauni, a family register extract and the full list of heirs, and kept the receipt. At the hearing the order entered all heirs. Had they waited until after the order, they would have faced a long appeal.
