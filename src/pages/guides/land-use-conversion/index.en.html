Before building a house, shop or warehouse on farmland, or selling it as plots, many states require a change of land use. Depending on the state it is called diversion, non-agricultural (NA) permission, change of land use (CLU) or a section-based "non-agricultural declaration". Rules, fees and officers vary by state; below is a general overview, not legal advice.

Check three things first

  1. Land category in the record: how the land is classified in the khatauni/jamabandi/7-12. Some categories (such as government leases or land under special laws) may bar conversion or sale.
  2. Master plan/zoning: if the land is near a town, check what use the development authority's master plan assigns to that area. If the zone doesn't fit, permission is hard.
  3. Clear title: name recorded, mutation complete, no dispute or mortgage.

General process

  1. Apply for change of land use on the state portal or at the tehsil/SDM/Collector office (in urban areas, the development authority).
  2. Usually attach: current land record, map/site plan, sale deed, ID, and other no-objections as the state requires.
  3. Inquiry and site inspection by the revenue/town planning department.
  4. Payment of the conversion fee/premium as per state rules.
  5. After the order, the category change is entered in the revenue record. Keep a certified copy of the order.

For plot buyers

  • Ask the seller for the land-use conversion order and a copy; don't rely on verbal claims like "the colony is approved".
  • Plotted colonies may also need development authority or RERA registration; check the state's official site.
  • See where roads, drains and public spaces are shown on the layout.

Risks without permission

  • Building plan not approved; trouble with power/water connections.
  • Banks refusing a loan or not accepting the property as security.
  • Risk of penalties, notices or action against construction.
  • Buyers backing out when you resell.

Example

Someone bought "plots" carved out of farmland near a highway. The registry went through, but when applying for a building plan they learned the land was still agricultural and the master plan marked the area as a green belt. Checking the record category and master plan before buying would have shown the risk.
